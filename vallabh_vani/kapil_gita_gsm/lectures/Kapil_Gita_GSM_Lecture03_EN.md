# 4. Utpatti-pakṣa and Upapatti-pakṣa: the Whiteboard, the Garland and the Spice That Is Lacking

*[00:12:01]*

**Speaker:** *Jñāna-śaktyāpi utpatti-pakṣe utpattiḥ, anyathā pralayaḥ.* And in *jñāna-śakti* also there are *utpatti-pakṣa* and *upapatti-pakṣa*. *Utpatti-pakṣa* means where you are aware of the what is being produced. And *upapatti-pakṣa* means you are not aware of the produced production, but all the items which are produced, if you can see in the original form of it, as a either concealed, hidden, or let us say unrevealed — if you can perceive that, then it is called *upapatti-pakṣa*.

For instance, I am showing you this whiteboard. Now, number of the factors you are perceiving while perceiving this whiteboard: the plain surface, the frame, base, the corner socket. All this you are perceiving in the perception of this whiteboard as an *anugata*. But sometime what happens: we can perceive this as a made of all these factors, and sometime we perceive it that's not made of these factors but constituted of this factor. So there they appear as a part of the whiteboard. But if you have a little awareness, that in first glance you can see that this whiteboard is made of how many items. So anything which can be, which we perceive, has a two types of perception: *utpatti-pakṣa* and *upapatti-pakṣa*.

Likewise Brahman: in the awareness, knowledge of the Brahman also — the Brahman is cause of this universe. So if somebody can perceive the universe hidden in the Brahman, but not being produced, then it is *upapatti-pakṣa*. But someone immediately can predict: oh, because Brahman has all this element of *nāma*, *rūpa*, *karma* and all that, *kāla*, *karma*, *svabhāva*, *prakṛti*, *puruṣa*, so world is made of this much factors from the Brahman — then it is called *utpatti-pakṣa*. Means you consider them as a not constituting factor but the part of the construction.

Many time, even doctor, carpenter, in a first glance they can say: this is defect, or this is not perfect. How? Because they consider, they perceive everything from the *utpatti-pakṣa*. They can immediately recognize why this problem happened: because while constructing, this such mistake is made. And in the ‹*upapatti-pakṣa*› you don't see such phenomena out of which it is constructed, but out of which it is constituted. So there is a little difference between constitution and construction. So anything can be perceived either from the point of view of *utpatti-pakṣa* or from the point of view of *upapatti-pakṣa*. They sound to be contradictory, though, but such contradiction is there in all the perception. People are generally not aware.

For instance, if I suddenly ask: how many flowers in this garland? You have seen the garland, but not the flower. You just check with yourself: you have, you have seen the garland with all the flowers, and you have taken it as a *upapatti*. But the person who knows, in a first glance — we say, oh, this garland is made of this many flower; then it is *utpatti-pakṣa*. Am I clear?

Many time it happens: some new items is prepared by our wife, mother or anybody, cook. The person who is knowledgeable, immediately, by first taste, he can say: this is lacking, this is excess. But a person like me always feel some, some *gaḍbaḍ* is there, but I cannot pinpoint tell what is lacking, what spice is lacking in this. So this is *utpatti-pakṣa* and *upapatti-pakṣa*: in painting also, in garland also, in furniture also. Carpenter can immediately tell what is lacking in this, why it is not functioning well. An engineer can tell you, but a driver of car cannot immediately tell you what's wrong with car. So that's ‹*utpatti-pakṣa*› and ‹*upapatti-pakṣa*›: are same, they refer the same object, but with different view. Two different view of the same object.

# Brief Summary

**Content.** The session completes the twenty-fourth chapter. Kardama's praise of the God in Devahūti's womb (3.24.33) is read with the Subodhinī as a proof that Kapila is Akṣara Brahman and therefore omniform: *para* is *akṣara*, *pradhāna* is *prakṛti*, *puruṣa* consciousness, *mahat* the blueprint of the universe, *kāla* the agitator of the *guṇas*, *kavi* the seer who owns *mahat*, *trivṛt* the universal self-awareness, the *lokapālas* the gods; these are his causal forms, and the *prapañca* held in his self-awareness is his effect form. The speaker draws out two tools of analysis: *anugama*, the factors silently present in every perception, and the two views of anything known, *utpatti-pakṣa*, which sees how a thing is constructed, and *upapatti-pakṣa*, which sees what it is constituted of; Brahman is thus both *prapañca-rūpa* and *niṣprapañca-rūpa*, as in the Siddhānta Muktāvalī, and Kapila holds both *jñāna-śakti* (Sāṅkhya, theory) and *kriyā-śakti* (Yoga, *divya-dṛṣṭi*). Kapila's own words to Kardama (3.24.36–37) then give the purpose of the incarnation: to free seekers from *durāśaya*, the confusion in which the self cannot tell itself from body, senses and elements, by the enumeration of the *tattvas*. Enumeration needs classification, naming (*uddeśa*) and defining (*lakṣaṇa*); interdependence, which the Buddhist logician takes as a mark of falsity, Mahāprabhuji takes as a mark of intrinsic relation. Sāṅkhya runs from the unanalysed one to the analysed many, the reverse of the Upaniṣadic synthesis, and leaves the self as the counter rather than the countable. The path was lost by long forgetfulness, neither new nor merely old, and Kapila took a body to revive it.
