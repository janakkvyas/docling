# 4. Arthāśrayatva: Artha as Intent, and Plato's Shadows in the Pit

*[00:13:28]*

**Speaker:** So *śabdasya lakṣaṇāny āha arthāśrayatvam iti. Artho hi śabdam āśritya tiṣṭhati*‹श्रवणाधारित›. *Artho hi śabdam āśritya tiṣṭhati. Arthasya ghaṭa-paṭādeḥ rūpa-trayam*‹श्रवणाधारित›. Now here *artha* means object as well as the meaning. Meaning also has same meaning: "What do you mean?" means "What is your intention?" So many of the materialists consider that consciousness is produced like a fermentation, effervescence, of the matter. We don't share that idea. We consider that matter is an expression of the consciousness. We don't consider matter as merely idea, like idealist philosopher.

I may give you one clarification from the point of view of the Platonic theory. Plato was of opinion that this entire universe is made of idea, and all that exist as an idiot — not idiot. There's a little difference between idiot and ideate: that which corresponds to idea is ideate. So that ideate is nothing but the shadow of the idea. That was Plato's thought, and he gave a very apt example for it. And he says: suppose there is some person thrown into a deeper pit of earth, where he cannot come out. But the person or the things that moving around the periphery of that pit produce the shadow, and he cannot see whose shadow it is. So he would consider shadow as the real thing. That is the object of consciousness. Because in the pit he cannot see the persons who are moving around the periphery of the pit, so he cannot form the idea of what the real figure can be of which shadow is coming into the pit. But we know — those who are outside the pit — that it is merely a shadow, and the real phenomena is the person or object. So Plato‹?› consider that all the reality — God has idea of all this universe, and what we are seeing is the shadow of that idea, because we are in pit of darkness. So we consider the shadow as a reality. But shadows are merely shadow; the reality is the idea, of which the entire universe is shadow. So please try to distinguish this idea of the ideate. Vallabha Vedānta doesn't share this.

Though Plato does not believe in the idealism. The scholars of the Greek philosophy consider Plato as not idealist, but ideist, or ideal — not in the sense of that which is a product of the idea, but ideal in the sense of "this is an ideal watch". Ideal watch means that which perfect watch. So imperfect world is an imperfect phenomena, and the idea of God is always perfect. And for Plato, even God himself was an idea, a unified idea of the entire universe. So the God is idea; under that idea there are infinite ideas, and these infinite ideas has ideate, which we are perceiving, which are imperfect. So they are imperfect. Because they are imperfect, they are produced, they are destroyed, they are different, they show the difference, distinct character. But in the God they are not different — I mean, totally not different — and they are imperishable. So ideally, all this universe is imperishable; but the ideate, being imperfect, are perishable and producible. Because they are imperfect shadow of the idea that is lying — that is there in the idea, that is there as a part of the idea of God.

It has some resemblance to our contention, that originally Brahman was a *satyaṁ jñānam*. Instead of using the word idea, we have used the word *jñānam*, knowledge. Brahman is the truth, knowledge and infinity. So the entire world is an outcome of that true, infinite, true knowledge. So infinite true knowledge has taken the many various forms and the things, and we are a specimen of that knowledge — imperfect specimen. We do not use the term imperfection or perfection. But because even Plato uses this term, imperfection, perfection, in the sense of which is destroyable in time and which is not destroyable in time — which is in opposition of the ideal, real.

# Brief Summary

**Content.** The session takes up only two stanzas of the twenty-sixth adhyāya, 3.26.33–34, chosen because they ground the verbal testimony that Vedānta must justify epistemologically and metaphysically; the rest of the section, which falls under perception and inference, is passed over. Brahman is presented as the Vedāntic grand unification, and creation as issuing from sound, with parallels from the Bible and the Qur'ān. Sound is argued to have a substantial and not merely attributive character. Under *arthāśrayatvam*, *artha* is read as meaning or intent: the object is an objectification of consciousness, and its *ādhidaivika* form — the concept — rests in *śabda*, while its *ādhibhautika* form rests in clay or metal and its *ādhyātmika* form in knowledge, will and effort; Plato's ideas and ideates, and the contrast of ideal and real, are set beside this and distinguished from it, and the four levels of speech and the coinage Sat Śrī Akāl illustrate how different words carry one intent. Under *draṣṭur liṅgatvam*, consciousness divides itself into observer, observable and observation, which arise simultaneously and are unified in observation; this is not idealism, since only the apparent externality of the object is denied; and a word heard indicates both the thing and the knower who speaks it. Under *tanmātratvam*, sound is the subtle form and space the gross form through which sound passes; space is a positive category, against the European and Buddhist views of it as absence, with the Upaniṣad's derivation of the elements from *ākāśa*. Verse 34 defines space by its function: giving room to beings, making inner and outer, and supporting *prāṇa*, *indriya* and *antaḥkaraṇa*, the four elements being its sheath, in three tiers. The session closes on scripture as Brahman in the form of sound, to be discussed later, and announces the Nalakūbara text for the following sittings.
