# 2. Saṅkalpa, Vikalpa and Kāma; Aniruddha, Lord of the Senses

# 5. Buddhi from the Taijasa Ahaṅkāra: From Metaphysics to Epistemology

*[00:18:17]*

Hereonward *buddhi* comes, intellect. And with this intellect we are entering from the metaphysics to the epistemology. The entire epistemology of Vallabha Vedānta has its roots in this description of the intellect. And there are two *ślokas* ascribe to it. So let us discuss those.

> तैजसात्तु विकुर्वाणाद्बुद्धितत्त्वमभूत्सति ।  
> द्रव्यस्फुरणविज्ञानमिन्द्रियाणामनुग्रहः ॥
>
> *taijasāt tu vikurvāṇād buddhi-tattvam abhūt sati |*  
> *dravya-sphuraṇa-vijñānam indriyāṇām anugrahaḥ ||* (Śrīmad Bhāgavata 3.26.29)
>
> [Intellect is a product of the *taijasa* aspect, being disturbed; *buddhi* grasps the being; it reflects the *dravya*, substance, it is specific knowledge, and it obliges the motor organ and sense organ.]

And *buddhi*, intellect, is a product of the *rājasa* aspect of *ahaṅkāra* — sorry, *taijasa*, *rājasa* aspect of the mind. So *vikāra utpanna*: so when it is — when *rājasa man* is disturbed, it produces the function of *buddhi*, intelligence. *Buddhi*, intellect, grasps the being. This is very vital concept of the *buddhi*: *buddhi-tattvam abhūt sati*. *Sati* means that which has a being. And *dravya-sphuraṇa-vijñānam*: and it reflects — ‹…› — it reflects: *buddhi*, intellect, reflects the *dravya*, substance. *Vi*— *vijñānam*: specific knowledge. And *indriyāṇām anugrahaḥ*: and it obliges the motor organ and sense organ. Because if you want to do — if you want to act with any of your motor organ, your intellect assisted: it is on account of the assistance provided by the intellect. And if you want to understand any stimuli which is supplied to you through the sense, sense organ, like eye, ear, etcetera, so the correct assessment is made by intellect. That's why it — it obliges both, sense organ and motor organ.

> संशयोऽथ विपर्यासो निश्चयः स्मृतिरेव च ।  
> स्वाप इत्युच्यते बुद्धेर्लक्षणं वृत्तितः पृथक् ॥

>
> [These are the five functions: doubt, illusion, determination, memory and the dream. Though there are five functions, *buddhi* is common one.]

Now five functions of the intellect are enumerated: *saṁśaya*, *viparyāsa*, *niścaya*, *smṛti* and *svāpa*. *Iti buddher lakṣaṇaṁ vṛttitaḥ pṛthak*: these are the five functions. Though there are five functions, but *buddhi* is common one. So one substance behaves in five way.

# 6. Saṁśaya and the Degrees of Stimulation: The Torch with an Adjustable Focus

*[00:21:54]*

*Ādhibhautikaṁ vibhāga-nirūpaṇa*… let us come to Subodhinī. *Ādhibhautikaṁ vibhāga-nirūpaṇenaiva lakṣayati saṁśayeti. Dravya-sphuraṇa-tāratamyād buddher nānāvidhatvam. Saṁskāra-tejasos tulya-prakāśakatve viśeṣāsphūrtau sama-saṁśayaḥ, alpa-viśeṣa-sphūrtau utkaṭa-koṭika-saṁśayaḥ* ‹श्रवणाधारित›.

What is *saṁśaya*? So *saṁśaya* is described as a *dravya-sphuraṇa-tāratamyāt*. Because the stimulation of the object by object has a various degree; it — it happens in the various degrees: low, mediocre and high intensity. With the low intensity, mediocre intensity or the high intensity — on an account of that, *buddhi* also starts — intellect also start discriminating what is being perceived by the sense organ, and self-awareness, and the mind. *Dravya-sphuraṇa-tāratamyāt*.

Now you can understand this by — nowadays I don't have at present with me, but previously I had one torch, such that — that it can — you can decrease or increase this intensity of focus by revolving left or right. So when the focus is sharp, of light beam, these object stimulates your eyes powerfully, more intensely. When focus is not that much sharp, ‹…› then object does not stimulate with that intensity as it reflects with the hyper-intensity. For instance, you can understand: this — but there is already light on the wall, and you are seeing some patches also, dark patches. But if I focus light — see the difference. Intensity decrease, increase. So what is being illuminated by this ordinary light is covered by the powerful light focus. The patches, the dark patches, are not removed, but they do not appear here in screen. But with eye you can see there more clearly. And if I don't throw light on those patches, in this light they are not that much clearly apparent; and if I show this through the light, you can observe all the details. So intellect behaves in this manner of light. And therefore intellect's divine name is Pradyumna. ‹*Dyumna*›‹?› means that which ‹illuminates›: the son of Kṛṣṇa.

So *saṁskāra-tejasos tulya-prakāśakatve*. Now two things play their role in — in — in the first function of the intellect, *saṁśaya*, doubt, as a two variety, *utkaṭa-koṭika* and *sama-koṭika*: mild doubt and sharp doubt. What is the difference between mild doubt and sharp doubt? Whether A or not A: that's a mild doubt. Mild doubt concerns with the one object only, but — it is there or it is not there. And ‹sharp doubt› has the alternative. There are not necessarily two alternative; there can be several alternatives, like A, B, C, D. So whether it is A or B or C or D: here you have a *utkaṭa-koṭika saṁśaya*. Because the other category — you are not simply are doubting A as presence, but in contrast to it you are doubting that: is it so that it is not A but it is a B? So it become *utkaṭa-koṭi*. Here you have to choose between A and not A, and here you have to choose between A or B. Like that. So that is called *utkaṭa-koṭi* and *anutkaṭa-koṭi*.

So *utkaṭa-koṭika saṁśaya* has some element, some germinal element of illusion. But still it is not illusion, because it is coming as a candidate of doubt, member of doubt. In illusion it not — it doesn't come as a candidate of doubt, but it come as a powerful alternative: when you perceive rope as a snake. But if you consider, is it a rope or is it snake, then it is doubt, *utkaṭa-koṭika* doubt.

So *saṁskāra-tejasos tulya-prakāśakatve*: your impression, past impression of the object, and the light — when they are equal, then you cannot discriminate the particular aspect of the object which you are perceiving. So there is a *sama-koṭi saṁśaya*: whether it is there or it is not there. *Alpa-viśeṣa-sphūrtau*: but when you are not sure of the — because here in *utkaṭa-koṭika saṁśaya*, A — you are quite certain that something appear as a A, but still you have a doubt whether it is A or not. But in *tulya*‹?› *utkaṭa-koṭika*, you are not even sure of the property of the A or B or C or D. So then it becomes *utkaṭa-koṭika saṁśaya*, which has a germinal alternative of doubtful presence of the B, C, D. Then comes the *vipar*—

# Brief Summary

**Content.** The session reads Śrīmad Bhāgavata 3.26.27–30 with Mahāprabhuji's Subodhinī. The mind arises from the *sāttvika* (*vaikārika*) aspect of *ahaṅkāra* through a disturbance of the *guṇas* that time keeps up as God's power; the Subodhinī rejects the view, suggested by the *śruti* "*annamayaṁ hi somya manaḥ*", that food is its material cause. Mind is defined by its nature (*saṅkalpa* and *vikalpa*), its effect (it gives rise to *kāma*), its presiding deity Aniruddha (controller of the senses, to be countered only by *bhagavad-upāsanā*) and its physical aspect, which *yogīs* can master only slowly. The epithet *śāradendīvara-śyāma* leads into the shades of meaning of *śyāma* and the meanings of colours in different cultures. With *buddhi*, born of the *taijasa* aspect, the speaker moves from metaphysics to epistemology: *buddhi* grasps being, reflects substance, assists the senses and has five functions, namely doubt, illusion, determination, memory and sleep. He separates *sama-koṭika* doubt, which only asks whether a thing is there or not, from *utkaṭa-koṭika* doubt, which suggests alternatives and already holds the seed of illusion, and he explains the *atha* that marks the move from double-mindedness to single-mindedness. Since a school's theory of illusion shows its foundations, he sets out the *khyāti* debate: where the malfunction occurs, Prabhākara's *akhyāti*, the objective classification of the object as *sat*, *asat*, *sadasat* or *sadasad-vilakṣaṇa*, *sat-khyāti*, Śaṅkara's *anirvacanīya-khyāti*, the Naiyāyika *anyathā-khyāti* and the Buddhist *ātma-khyāti* or *asat-khyāti*.
