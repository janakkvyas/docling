# 2. The Third Skandha as Sarga: Bondage and Liberation Both Created

*[00:04:42]*

And Śrīmad Bhāgavata — beside that, one point which should be understood thoroughly is that this Kapila Gītā comes in this Skandha, Third Skandha of Bhāgavata, which is supposed to be the narration of the creation, *sarga*. And in *sarga* normally, beside Mahāprabhuji, everybody considers *sarga* means bondage, and liberation means escaping from the *sarga* process, *sṛṣṭi* process. To certain extent that is true, because they just go in opposite direction: liberation process is the reverse of the *sarga*, and *sarga* process is the reverse of the liberation.

And so, others are not so particular about emphasizing this point. But Mahāprabhuji ‹is› quite clear in his mind that when he means creation, creation means the creation of the Richard‹?› who are in bondage of *avidyā* and the creation of those who are not in the bondage, *mukti*. And that *muktas* are also created. Now it just goes against the popular concept of the bondage and the liberation, because normal concept is this: that bondage can be cancelled because it is created, and liberation cannot be cancelled because liberation is not created, but liberation just occurs. So it's not a process of the creation, but it is a reverse of the creation. But Mahāprabhuji says that the souls who are in bondage and the souls who are liberated, both are the creation of God, and God's creation involves both this.

# 3. Linear and Cyclical Accounts of Creation

*[00:07:55]*

To certain extent almost all the religions have this type of narration of the creation, but they are not clearly emphasizing this point. For instance, in Judaism and Christianity as well as in the Qur'ān, there are *fariśtā* who are not created in the process of creation. And then there is a problem, theological problem: if they are perennial, uncreated, what is their status? Can they be controlled by God or not? So they have to say that God controls them, because God is almighty; and then *fariśtā* are not almighty, angels are not almighty, they are slave of God.

And Christianity particularly, in the narration of the creation, says that Adam was also originally a creation, immortal creation by God. But because Adam did not follow the commandment of the God, so God cursed him to be mortal. And since God cursed him for mortality, God cursed him also that "now you will produce your progeny out of sex, and that sex is sin; therefore your product, your birth involves this sin." And your liberation will be — for Judaism, if you follow the Ten Commandments; for Christianity, if you identify yourself with the crucified Christ, particularly the Catholic Christianity, not all these twenty-seven sects of the Christianity. And even Mohammedans also, Islam also says that if you follow the orders, the commandments given through the Muhammad to the public at large, then you are liberated ‹the speaker's characterization›.

So immortality is there, because liberation from the creative process is an escaping from the creative process; so there is no birth, so there is no rebirth also, and you get immortality. But physical mortality is there of the body, but soul, self by itself is immortal. That is their narration of the creation. In any case it is not a sport, sportly creation; it is a very serious creation. And it happen in the linear fashion: that once it was created and once it will be destroyed, and that grows in a straight line from creation to the destruction of this universe.

We do not have such concept; we do not share this concept. For us creation — for Jainism, let it be Jainism, Buddhism or Vedicism, all the three religion consider this creation in a cyclical process. So since it is considered cyclical process — it is not linear concept, but it is cyclical concept. And in that cyclical concept, for ‹Jains› and Buddhist, ‹it› may run to several causes and conditions. And even in the Vedānta or the Vedic philosophy, that cycle may be caused by *karma* or some other reasons.

# 4. Creation as the Sport of God: the Ten Topics and the Definition of Brahman

*[00:13:04]*

But particularly Bhāgavata describes the process of creation as a sportly action of the God. It was an extension of the self, duplication. So when God involves in the self-duplication, self-multiplication — and that multiplication is not due to the inevitable nature of God, but due to the desirable sport by God. So it's a sportly creation. And in that sportly creation, Bhāgavata narrates the process of this entire cosmic process; cosmic process is narrated under the ten headings, *sarga, visarga, sthāna, poṣaṇa, ūti, manvantara, īśānukathā, nirodha, mukti* and *āśraya*:

> अत्र सर्गो विसर्गश्च स्थानं पोषणमूतयः ।  
> मन्वन्तरेशानुकथा निरोधो मुक्तिराश्रयः ॥  
>
> *atra sargo visargaś ca sthānaṁ poṣaṇam ūtayaḥ |*  
> *manvantareśānukathā nirodho muktir āśrayaḥ ||* (Śrīmad Bhāgavata 2.10.1)
>
> [*Sarga, visarga, sthāna, poṣaṇa, ūti, manvantara, īśānukathā, nirodha, mukti* and *āśraya*.]

Now roughly, Brahman is described: *yato vā imāni bhūtāni jāyante, yena jātāni jīvanti, yat prayanty abhisaṁviśanti, tad vijijñāsasva, tad brahma* (Taittirīya Upaniṣad 3.1). So Brahman is described as the creator, the sustainer and the destroyer, or liberator, of the soul and the world. So these four functions of the Brahman, very significant function, which no other can share with God. These are the attributes of God, rather, in a monopolistic way: no other can create this universe, no other can sustain this universe, no other can destroy this universe, and no other can provide the liberation to the soul in this universe. And whatever we can think as a reason for creation or sustenance or the destruction or the liberation of the soul or world, these are all the reasons as if different functions of God, but not independent function from the God.

So this, our entire universe, is a sportly action of the God. *Sa ekākī na ramate, sa dvitīyam aicchat* (cf. Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 1.4.3): he was not enjoying his solitude, so he desired the multiplication, multitudinous, and he multiplied himself. That was self-multiplication. And since it is a self-multiplication, that desire does not involve the wants of any other than itself. So there is nothing lacking in the Brahman which compels Brahman to create this world; yet it creates.

# 5. Nature or Desire: the Example of Paralysis, and the Fullness of Brahman

*[00:16:46]*

So there are two possibilities of the creative process: either it is on account of the unchangeable nature, or on account of the sportly desire. Anything that occurs because of one's unchangeable nature cannot be considered or regarded as a sport. For instance, in paralysis our hands start shaking. Now it happens because of the disease nature; that shaking of the hand in the paralysis cannot be regarded as a sport of the person, because it does not involve the ‹volition›. So world has not come out of the Brahman because of the pressure of his causative nature, unsuppressible causative nature; but world has been created because he desired to be many. And that makes this creation as a sportly creation, because Brahman did not ‹want›, Brahman was not deprived of anything, because Brahman is considered as a *pūrṇa*, full.

What is fullness? Fullness in existence, fullness in consciousness and fullness in the blissfulness.

> पूर्णमदः पूर्णमिदं पूर्णात्पूर्णमुदच्यते ।  
> पूर्णस्य पूर्णमादाय पूर्णमेवावशिष्यते ॥  
>
> *pūrṇam adaḥ pūrṇam idaṁ pūrṇāt pūrṇam udacyate |*  
> *pūrṇasya pūrṇam ādāya pūrṇam evāvaśiṣyate ||* (Īśa Upaniṣad, invocation)
>
> [This is complete, full; that is full. Full has come out of the full, and even after coming out of the full, fullness remain full.]

That was the message of the Upaniṣad for Brahman. Now fullness is a synonym for Brahman. Brahman is a fullness: fullness of existence, consciousness and blissfulness. So if it is full, so it does not lack or ‹is not› deprived of anything. So if fullness is doing something, it has to be — and if it is doing something voluntarily, it cannot be anything other than this sport.

# 6. Sarga, Visarga, Sthāna, Poṣaṇa and Ūti: the Cap and the Shoes, the Policeman and the Nurse

*[00:19:30]*

So *sarga* is *līlā*. *Sarga, visarga*: *sarga* is *līlā*. Creation of those material which are used in the creation, creation of those material, raw material, which are used for the manufacturing, is *sarga*. *Visarga* means *viśeṣa sarga*: so then raw material is refined into the desired form; that is *visarga*. Then comes the *sthāna*, placement. When something is created, manufactured, where it should be placed. So the way, the place and the mode of placing, placement, ultimately culminates into the nature of the created thing.

For instance, if you use cap to be put on your head, the placement of cap on the head makes cap ‹with› little delicacy, with delicacy. And you use the shoes to wear in foot; that naturally, by default, be rough, devoid of delicacy. Because it is using — as we can see that in our skin also: that skin becomes rough in our foot; the skin does not become rough in our head, unless some disease occurred, skin disease. So the way you use, it acquires the nature, and where you put. So placement is the reason of the created nature, created thing.

So *sthāna, poṣaṇa*: then the nourishment. After placement one has to maintain them accordingly, and when it is maintained that way — *poṣaṇaṁ tad-anugrahaḥ* (Śrīmad Bhāgavata 2.10.4). Then comes the *ūti*. That creates the *vāsanā*: *vāsanā* not in the sense of lust, but *vāsanā* in the sense of inclination, aptitude. That generates your aptitude, where we are.

Suppose if somebody after education is placed as a police: so police job is a rough job; it will have an aptitude of the roughness. If somebody is placed in the hospital, nurse — nurses cannot afford to have a rough behaviour; they have to behave softly with the patients. So the nature of the nurse, ‹the› sister, will be delicate, soft, like that. You place someone as an advocate, it will develop the tendency of argumentation. And if you make somebody listen‹?›, so the man listen all, everybody; so that listener would naturally be a passive, ultimately, in aptitude, because he will develop the habit of listening everything; he will not be able to argue. So the nature is culminated on account of the placement and the maintenance of the person — that is, same person as well as the object also.

# 7. Manvantara, Īśānukathā, Nirodha, Mukti and Āśraya

*[00:23:57]*

*Ūtayaḥ*‹?›. Then comes the *manvantara*. *Manvantara* means duty. On the basis of your inclination and aptitude your duties decided. Because if you do not have that inclination or aptitude, and if some duties ‹are› imposed on you, then you will not be able to fulfil that duty. So then comes the *sad-dharma*: *manvantara*, *dharma*. And if you are observing your duty in the creation, then there is a chance, because that provides you the space to devote yourself to God.

So the ninth canto of Bhāgavata deals with the devotion. Devotion means the story of the devotees and divinities. And after devotion comes the *nirodha*. *Nirodha* means constraint. Constraint means: because of the devotion, you ‹are› constrained into the divine, divine personality, who is playing, whose play is this creation. So when he is constrained among you, you also become constrained in his personality. So that comes after the devotion. If you are not devoted to the Lord, playful Lord, then you would not be inclined to participate in this universe as one participate in sport. Though we are part of the sport, are we participating in the sport? Not necessarily. So what is required on your part? Being a *aṁśa*, part of the God, that you should participate in this sport, and wholeheartedly. That is what it mean by *nirodha*, which is the subject matter or theme of the Daśama Skandha.

And if you participate in the spirit in the sport of the God, then you get liberation, which is the theme of eleventh canto. And the twelfth canto describe the *āśraya*, *bhāvāpatti*: means again, after liberation, your cycle ends and you again become the primordial stuff of this player. So that's a cyclical sport.

# 8. Brahman, Paramātmā and Bhagavān: the Six Qualities of the Ruler

*[00:27:13]*

And as the Brahman is described as *brahmeti paramātmeti bhagavān iti śabdyate* (Śrīmad Bhāgavata 1.2.11), so it is a three-dimensional phenomena. It is a Brahma: Brahma means it excludes nothing, all-inclusive. Paramātman means that it is an immanent in all the creation. And Bhagavān. So he is a transcendental and immanent: from the point of view of Brahman he is transcendental, and from the point of view of Paramātmā he is immanent, and from the point of view of Bhagavān he is ruler also.

So ruler, who can rule? Who has *aiśvarya, vīrya, yaśas, śrī, jñāna* and *vairāgya*. That was the ideal concept of the ruler: that if somebody is ruler, he must have an *aiśvarya*, virility — *aiśvarya, vīrya, yaśas, śrī, jñāna* and *vairāgya* — that makes someone eligible to rule. So we have defined the ruler aspect of the God as a Bhagavān because of these six qualities.

So when he rules — although in the Upaniṣads the Brahman is described as a creator, sustainer, destroyer and the provider of liberation, but when the Bhagavān comes, corresponding to Bhagavān: *bhaga* means *aiśvarya, vīrya, yaśas, śrī, jñāna, vairāgya*. And the one who has this *aiśvarya, vīrya, yaśas, śrī, jñāna, vairāgya* is worthy to be worshipped. So since that who has all these six qualities is worthy to be worshipped, so the one who worships becomes the *bhakta*. So Bhagavān, devotion: the object of the devotion is a Bhagavān, and the subject of the devotion is *bhakta*. And why he is devoted? Because someone has an *aiśvarya, vīrya, yaśas, śrī, jñāna, vairāgya*; that makes the thing or phenomena as a object to be worshipped. So six qualities are enumerated as a characteristic of the lordly character of the Bhagavān, Brahman.

Therefore *aiśvarya* — *sarga, visarga*: Upaniṣad describes the Brahman in the fourfold way, creator, sustainer, destroyer and provider of liberation. But the aspect of the Bhagavān, of the Brahman, Paramātmā — the Brahman, Paramātmā: after *kṛta*‹?›, Brahma, this whole world has come out of the Brahma, and God dwells inside it, so he is a Paramātmā; and dwelling inside it, rules it also, being a Bhagavān. So those six qualities are narrated: *sthāna, poṣaṇa, ūti, manvantara, īśānukathā* and *nirodha*. That's a description of the Bhagavān. And *sarga, visarga* is an explanation of the creator God. And six‹?› canto, *adhyāyas*, are for the ruler God, and two *adhyāyas* are for the provider of liberation, liberator, and the destroyer God: so eleventh and tenth‹?›. Again two, *sarga, visarga*, and *mokṣa* and *āśraya*, *bhāvāpatti*: four. And in between six *adhyāyas*, six *skandhas*, six *skandhas* for describing that Brahman, Paramātmā, as a Bhagavān.

# 12. The Debt to the Forefathers: Marriage and Dawkins's Adamant Cell

*[00:39:45]*

So it was considered in those days that anyone who takes birth, he takes birth as owing to repay certain things to those who are reason of his birth. So three persons comes: *deva, ṛṣi* and *pitṛ*; *deva, ṛṣi* and *pitṛ*. Now *pitṛ* means forefathers. Because we are born in the family of our forefathers, so we owe certain — we are in debt with our forefathers. And how we can repay that debt? Because as we are born, so we have to make this process of birth and birth, continue the process of birth. And that's why Vedas say *tad martyasya amṛtatvaṁ yad vai prajā ajāyata* ‹श्रवणाधारित›. What is the immortality of the born that he gets? He is reborn as his progeny. *Tad amṛta*, *tad martyasya*: man is a mortal, but what can be the immortality of the mortal creature? That he produces this *prajā*: *prakarṣeṇa jāyate*. He remakes himself in the form of his progeny. And that we owe to our forefathers.

So please try to understand. In our scripture our marriage is not a contract, social contract. Our marriage is not a legal contract. There can be social contract, there can be legal contract; nor it is a religious contract. For us our marriage has some metaphysical reason.

And Richard Dawkins in his book described the adamant cell: the cell which is created once, it has an adamant, it wants to survive. And we know from the scientific point of view also that many a time this world has come to a dead end, where dinosaurs died, but the cell could not die. So new creation occurred: animals were created; before that fishes were there. So cell want to survive; cell is an adamant. We don't know, but scientists say. We think that we are descendants of the human being or monkey or whatever that may be; Darwin's theory is a very narrow perspective of the creation of the human being. Modern biology says that the first cell, living cell, which generated in ocean due to the chemical reaction of the water — when ultraviolet light passes through that chemical, it created a chemistry in the ocean water that a living cell produced. And that produced cell does not want to die. So even that cell is present in our body: the cell, biological cell, which has all metabolic functions.

What is metabolic function? That it absorbs from the surrounding; it absorbs, then it digest, it grows, it recreates and dies. That's a cycle; that goes on, goes on. The cell goes on creating itself, absorbing from the surrounding, growing, recreating and then dying. So we owe to that original cell also something. What do we owe? That we must not act in this universe in the manner that the environment which produces the cell, biological cell, get destroyed. Don't we owe that to that original cell? We do have, we owe this as a debt, and we have to repay that debt to maintain that environment on the surface of the earth, the environment that produced the first biological cell, protozoa.

Like that, our scripture says that we owe to our forefathers that we must continue the process of the birth. So it has a metaphysical function; our marriage is to fulfil that desire. It is not like that, what Marx says, that we have made female a machine of birth, "go on producing child". No, not necessarily. Because our *śāstra* does not share that idea that we must treat a female as a machine that produces child. No, she is not machine; she is your better half. *Ardhabṛgalam ivāyaṁ puruṣaḥ* ‹श्रवणाधारित› (cf. Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 1.4.3): she lacks the half — female lacks the half and males also lack the half — and they become complete, *tenāyaṁ pūryate* ‹श्रवणाधारित›: they become complete when they join with each other. And that joining, conjoining, is a metaphysical purpose of our creation. So we must continue, and we must repay the debt that we owed to our forefathers. That is first debt.

# 13. The Debt to the Ṛṣis: “Āye, Nahāye, Khāye aur Gaye” and the Twice-born

*[00:47:56]*

Second debt: we are educated, we have been taught. Because we communicate knowledge, information. All the creatures — forget about the men — all the creatures communicate their knowledge to their kids, their progeny, but they have a limitation there: how much they can convey. We have the *ṛṣis*, who encoded the knowledge in the scripture, and they taught us. And since they taught us the knowledge, so we are in debt to those *ṛṣi* also, and we can repay it only when we study the scripture that gives us a knowledge.

Because we are not a creature which simply gets birth, lives for few years and then die, producing. Normally in joking way it is said, *āye, nahāye, khāye aur gaye* [came, bathed, ate and went]. Means the bath for baby is given when he gets born, and then eats, and then again dies, and again bath is provided to the dead body, and then *gaye*. That's not the simplified story of the human life. Human life has also *dvija* concept. *Dvija* concept means twice-born: first he births out of the womb of his mother, and second birth is considered as the knowledge that he acquires. That's a second birth. And that knowledge is also to be Jīva‹?› duplicated and grown. So we have certain debt to our *ṛṣis*.

# 14. The Debt to the Devas: the Eight Devas and the Meaning of div

*[00:50:41]*

And thirdly, the divine factors, *deva*. Now who are the *devas*? Naturally question may be asked. Normally when we talk or think about *deva*, we have certain pictures in our mind, like *deva* Gaṇeśajī, Kārtikeya, Lakṣmī, Sarasvatī, Brahmā. They are secondary *devas*. Primary gods are considered as *bhūmir āpo 'nalo vāyuḥ khaṁ mano buddhir eva ca ahaṅkāra* (Bhagavadgītā 7.4): these are all *devas*, eight *devas*. Why they are considered *devas*? They are the matters, materials. Yeah, that's a way to look at it: you may consider *devas* as a matter, and there is an approach which consider matter as a *deva*. So we consider this as a *deva*. Why we call them *deva*? Because ‹divine play unfolds in waking, dreaming, and deep sleep states›‹?›.

Etymologically, *deva śabda* is constituted from the verb *div*: *dīvyatīti devaḥ*. And what is *dīvyati*? So *dīvyati* means — *div*, *krīḍā*, first of all: it's a play, whose behaviour is playful. *Vijigīṣā*: whose behaviour is not merely playful, but victorious play; we want to be victorious. *Krīḍā, vijigīṣā, moda*: and we want to register our victory not by jealousy or rivalry, but by *moda*, playfulness, enjoyableness, blissfulness. *Moda, mada*: and for that we have a self-awareness, *mada*. *Kānti*, luster, and *gati*, movement. So all these elements, wherever you find, this is *deva*. *Devas* are also considered *deva* because of this quality. And if we have these ten qualities, we are also there; and if we lack these qualities, we are *manuṣya*.

*Manuṣya* word is derived from the *man*, *jñāna*, *avabodhane*: understands. Merely understanding would not suffice in this creation, because this is a sport; so you have to be sportive. This is a sport; you should strive for the victory. So ‹krīḍā, vijigīṣā, moda›. And that your striving for victory should not be for rivalry or jealousy, but for *moda*, playfulness, joyfulness. *Svapna*, *kānti*: you must have a dream. You must not confine your activity to present, but the dream future. *Svapna, moda, mada, kānti, gatiṣu*. So when you show these traits in your behaviour, you become like a *deva*.

And our *ṛṣis* have seen this type of traits in all the materials, in the *pṛthvī*: ‹krīḍā›, *vijigīṣā, moda, mada, svapna, kānti, gati*, that I will explain. These all traits are there in all the matters, so they consider them as a *deva*. So we say thirty-three *deva*, thirty-three *devas*. Among the thirty-three *deva*, the enumeration number of the thirty-three *deva*, these are the *devas*, not the other *deva*; the other *devas* are the personification of these *devas*.

So Upaniṣad says, *agnir vāyur ādityas tisro devatāḥ* ‹श्रवणाधारित›: fire is a god, air is a god, sun is god. Why? Because fire has this type of *devatva*, *krīḍā, vijigīṣā, moda, mada, svapna, kānti, gati*. Air also has this element. One has to ‹have› vision to look at these elements, this phenomena, with such elements; and so Āditya. So these three *devas*, then thirty-three gods: multiply by this three. So we have a *deva-ṛṇa* also.

And that's why in Gītā Bhagavān says:

> देवान्भावयतानेन ते देवा भावयन्तु वः ।  
> परस्परं भावयन्तः श्रेयः परमवाप्स्यथ ॥  
>
> *devān bhāvayatānena te devā bhāvayantu vaḥ |*  
> *parasparaṁ bhāvayantaḥ śreyaḥ param avāpsyatha ||* (Bhagavadgītā 3.11)
>
> [*Devas* are providing you all that you want, but then you must also do accordingly.]

*Devas* are providing you all that you want, but then you must also do accordingly. You must not be destructive, and you must not be obligatory‹?›: "forget it, all this that you have gained" — you forget, and you do not maintain the proper decorum of God. Then what you will be making? This earth, this air, this fire, all polluted. And if you pollute this, who will die? They will not die; you will die. So you owe something to *deva* also. That was the concept of the *jāyamāno vai tribhir ṛṇavān jāyate, prajayā pitṛbhyo, vidyayā ṛṣibhyo, yajñena devebhyaḥ* ‹श्रवणाधारित› (cf. Taittirīya Saṁhitā 6.3.10.5).

# 15. Manu: Repay the Debts Before Mokṣa, and How India Became a Culture of Renunciation

*[00:57:31]*

And Manu says clearly:

> ऋणानि त्रीण्यपाकृत्य मनो मोक्षे निवेशयेत् ।  
> अनपाकृत्य मोक्षं तु सेवमानो व्रजत्यधः ॥  
>
> *ṛṇāni trīṇy apākṛtya mano mokṣe niveśayet |*  
> *anapākṛtya mokṣaṁ tu sevamāno vrajaty adhaḥ ||* (Manusmṛti 6.35)
>
> [Only after repaying your debt you should desire for liberation; if you make haste in this procedure, then you will not rise, but you will fall down.]

Only after repaying your debt you should desire for liberation. If you make haste in this procedure, then you will not rise but you will fall down: *anapākṛtya mokṣaṁ tu*, *anapākṛtya ṛṇāny etān mokṣam icchan pataty adhaḥ* (the speaker's wording of the second line). So this is a system, this is a system that God has playfully created in this universe, and that is being described here: *ṛṇatraya-parityāgo mokṣārthaṁ tasya varṇyate*. So three debts must be repaid, then *mokṣa*: you become eligible for *mokṣa*.

If before that you strive for *mokṣa* — actually, before that striving for *mokṣa* is not our concept. It is a concept proposed by the Jains and Buddhists. As a matter of fact, in their life sketch, if we see, they also went through this process: they also went through the studying process, they also went through the marrying process, they also went through the producing child process and acquiring knowledge process, and then they become *sannyāsa*. But in their preaching they started inspiring people at large: "Take the path of liberation immediately. Don't go, don't fall in this repayment process." And from then onward in our India, our culture become renunciation, culture of renunciation ‹the speaker's characterization›. Otherwise our culture was not renunciation; our culture was of *dharma, artha, kāma, mokṣa*, fourfold *puruṣārtha*. Our pursuits were not confined only to *mokṣa*. Our pursuits were confined to first of all your duty, duty to repay your debt; *artha, kāma*; and then fourth stage it comes as a *mokṣa*. This is how the *mokṣa* system is made. And the three pursuits keeps you in bondage, and fourth pursuit uplifts you for the liberation, escape from this bondage. This is the whole set-up.

# Brief Summary

**Content.** The opening session explains why the syllabus of Vallabha Vedānta takes Kapila Gītā adhyāyas 24–26 with the Subodhinī: the university asked for yoga, the Sāṅkhya categories run parallel to the Sarvanirṇaya Prakaraṇa of the Tattvārthadīpanibandha, and the story chapter gives the setting. The speaker places the Kapila Gītā in the Third Skandha as *sarga* and sets out Mahāprabhuji's distinctive view that both bondage and liberation are created, contrasting the linear, "serious" creation of the Semitic religions with the cyclical view of Jainism, Buddhism and the Vedic tradition and with the Bhāgavata's creation as the sport of a full Brahman that lacks nothing. He explains the ten topics of the Bhāgavata (*sarga* to *āśraya*), their relation to Brahman, Paramātmā and Bhagavān and to the six qualities of the ruler, the plan of the syllabus and the point where Mahāprabhuji's Subodhinī stops, and the image of creation as a maze with an entry and an exit, Kapila being the hero of *mokṣa-sṛṣṭi* who strives for liberation and teaches it to his mother.
