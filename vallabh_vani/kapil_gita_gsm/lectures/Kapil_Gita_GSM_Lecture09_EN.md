# 1. The Twenty-four Categories and the Place of Kāla

*[00:00:01]*

**Speaker:** As we have seen, that there are twenty-four categories, categories of the nature, natural phenomena — five, five, four, four, five, five, four and ten — whether *kāla* is a separate category, or *kāla* should be included among these twenty-four categories only. There were two or three opinions given. First opinion was that *kāla*, time — which is an equilibrium of all the attributes, *sattva*, *rajas*, *tamas* — and when it becomes operative, that the initial stage of operativeness is called ‹*kāla*›. Another opinion was given, that time is a *ceṣṭā* — *ab*, Bhagavān *aur puruṣa* [now, of Bhagavān and *puruṣa*]. In any case, time becomes operative when the primordial phenomena started vibrating for creation. Then comes the — because the equilibrium is disturbed by the time.

Now here one point is to be noted clearly: that when substance begins to undergo some changes, transformation, that is time, as a first opinion. Second: when the equilibrium of the nature, unmanifested, gets disturbed because of the infusion of the consciousness into matter, that is time. So time begins at that stage, and time comes into being at that stage. Before that, time is not. This is according to the Sāṅkhya *prakriyā* of Kapila.

# 2. Two Sāṅkhyas from One Kapila: Many Teachers, Many Theories, and *Mama Satyam*

*[00:03:27]*

But, as I have said, Sāṅkhya — Kapila has propagated both sort of Sāṅkhyas: *nirīśvara sāṅkhya* as well as *īśvara sāṅkhya*. In the Western scholarship there is one problem: if the two opinions are propagated or propounded, they consider there must be two persons, because two opinions cannot be of a same person. Such is not the case of India. Either you consider Buddha: Buddha Bhagavān propagated four different theories simultaneously. Kapila also propagated two theories, of *seśvara* and *nirīśvara*. Like there, many other *ṛṣis* who propagated two, or sometime more than two, three theories. If you consider Veda: Veda propagated so many numbers of theories at a time. Because we have a age-old custom that one system does not suffice for all the eligible seekers. *Na hy ekasmād guror jñānaṁ ‹su-sthiraṁ syāt› su-puṣkalam* (Śrīmad Bhāgavata 11.9.31) [from one teacher alone knowledge does not become firm and abundant]. One teacher cannot give you the abundant knowledge. So many gurus. But many gurus also have propagated many theories.

And for your information, even Śaṅkarācārya is considered as a *ṣaṇmata-pratiṣṭhāpanācārya*: he propagated six opinions at a time. In Purāṇa, Vyāsa — Vyāsa has written Śaiva Purāṇa, Vaiṣṇava Purāṇa, Śākta Purāṇa, and eighteen Purāṇas with different opinions. So to consider the eighteen Vyāsas is not our tradition. One *ṛṣi* can — *nṛṇāṁ prakṛti-vaicitryāc citrā vācaḥ sravanti*, *sravanti* ‹श्रवणाधारित› (cf. Śrīmad Bhāgavata 11.14.7–8) [from the variety of men's natures, varied words flow forth]. Because man is of a different, different natures, so one medicine does not suffice to all the men.

But somehow the monotheistic concept of religion goes against this presumption. Though they oppose this presumption, but among all the monotheistic religion there are so many denominations, subdivisions. For instance, Christianity has twenty-seven denominations; Islam was supposed to have a seventy-two denominations; and like that, even Judaism also has numbers of denomination. But unfortunately they fight with each other ‹the speaker's characterization›. Because they consider — there is a word in Pāṇini, though it is not nowadays in the usage: the synonym for war or conflict is *mama satyam*. Truth is my monopoly: that is considered as a synonym for war and conflict. *Mama satyam* — *mama satyam* means war, fight. *Satyam* is on my side, *satyam* is not your side.

We never thought that way. We consider one phenomena — since one phenomena has taken various forms, and those various forms cannot be described by one single person, even if one tries to describe them, so various scholars describe it very well in various ways: *ekaṁ sad viprā bahudhā vadanti* (Ṛgveda 1.164.46) [the one reality the wise speak of in many ways]. Normally people cut this half. Many time, in opposition to this, they ‹…› say that it's a blind-elephant simile: that those who describe in various way cannot be one person's description, but blinds cannot see the whole; therefore each blind describes the elephant in a manner in which he is capable to grab his limbs, like foot or ear or trunk or tail or whatever, so and so forth. Veda does not believe in this *andha-hasti-nyāya*. It is called *ekaṁ sad viprā bahudhā vadanti*. So *vipra* means those who are scholar, those who are seers, those who have realized: they describe the truth in various ways.

So somehow we in India had a feeling: the truth is one, but it manifest in various ways. And since it manifested in various ways, so the description — variety is in the description — is not an indication of the description of the blind, but the seer.

# 3. Many Theories of the Cosmos, and a Dispensary of Many Medicines

*[00:11:04]*

And perhaps you will agree with me that in the history of all the science, all the branches of science, number of scientists has described the same scientific theory in number of ways. For instance, for the universe, this cosmos: how many theories we have at present? Big Bang theory, continuously expanding theory, Pulsar theory, String theory, Creator‹?› theory. And it's not necessary that one theory is true, then other must be false, or other theory is true, this must be false. All this theory can be simultaneously become true, describing different facets of the same phenomena.

That's the truth with every single instance of every single perception. Any perception — take some not very popular thing, or animal, a bird. You place before the persons, five or six persons, and ask them what it is, and then answer will differ. Because how to perceive a thing must tally with each other — it's not necessary, as far as the essential reality of any phenomena is concerned. Because reality is a not monodimensional phenomena; it's a multidimensional phenomena. Because we perceive one dimension, and because we have a narrow mind, so we consider that the dimension which we perceive has to be true, and other dimensions, the appearance of the same reality in the other dimension, must be false. That's our narrowness of mind.

So some of the seers has described the same phenomena in various ways. That's a history, remarkable history of the philosophy and religion of India. That's why we have a *karma-mārga*, *jñāna-mārga*, *bhakti-mārga*, *tyāga-mārga*, *vairāgya-mārga*, *tapo-mārga*: various ways, of the God, forms of the God, various description of the same reality. So Kapila also has propagated two sorts of Sāṅkhya, and there is no contradiction in it, first of all.

Because a theory is propounded and preached and propagated for the benefit of those who cannot have a solution of their puzzle in life without this theory. And because the nature of men differs from each other, so — it differs — theories are also different. We cannot expect any doctor or dispensary to keep only one medicine. It's a doctor or dispensary: it must have many, many medicines, as per the many, many disease and the symptoms. Even one disease can have a number of symptoms; so to treat those symptoms, some variation in the same medicine also must occur. And this is this culture — call it philosophical culture, theological culture, or culture of India — is such that we never strive for one single theory, because one single theory never suffices to satisfy the intrinsic urge of the humankind, either for liberation or for non-liberation, or for survival, or for behaviour.

# 4. Kāla in the Vedāntic Sāṅkhya: the Quartz in the Watch

*[00:16:19]*

And since it is one part of the Akṣara Brahman, aspect of the Akṣara Brahman — so Akṣara Brahman is considered as *kāla*, *karma*, *svabhāva*, *prakṛti* and *puruṣa*, *pañcavidha*. And not necessarily *kāla* should be taken as a initiative vibration of matter, or the initiative vibration or the disturbance in the matter due to the consciousness, or the initiative action, initiative of action on the part of the *puruṣa*. But *kāla* is a system. *Kāla* is a system which is produced after this — which is produced, and which is an attribute of the primordial stuff which we call Akṣara Brahman: *kāla*, *karma*, *svabhāva*, *prakṛti* and *puruṣa*. But in *puruṣa*, in Akṣara Brahman, it remains unmanifested, and in world it becomes manifested. As quartz remains unmanifested in watch, but the movement of the hands of watch due to quartz is manifested: exact same way.

# 5. Citta as the Unconscious Mind: the Storeroom and the Showroom

*[00:18:18]*

Then comes — so when *kāla* disturbs the *prakṛti* — *prakṛti* is equilibrium, equilibrium of nature's attribute — then the infusion of the consciousness in *prakṛti* produces a phenomena which is called *citta*. Now what is *citta*? A universal *citta* means a universal mind, from the cosmic point of view. And from the individual point of view, you may, for the sake of clarity, understand the *citta* as a unconscious mind. There are three layers of mind: conscious, subconscious and unconscious. Unconscious mind is a *citta*, which is a storeroom for all the information that we take, receive, and we reproduce. Everything is stored there. It does not take an active part in the day-to-day behaviour, but it is treated as a *suṣupti-sākṣika*.

Because it is not a shouting phenomena, like ego. Ego is terribly shouting. Intellect is also shouting phenomena. *Manas*, mind, may not be that much shouting, but still it has some noise; it creates some noise. *Citta* remains quiet, because it stores. As any showroom is noisy, the storeroom has to be silent. We can see the silence, because once in a while people go there to bring some item and place in the showroom. So our intellect is like a showroom, and our ‹mind› is like a showroom of information that we produce or we receive — means inlet or outlet — and *citta* is a storeroom.

# 6. The Universal Citta as Blueprint: the Embryo, and the Conscious Variable

*[00:21:10]*

But the universal *citta* — means universal mind, not individual mind, universal mind — is considered as a blueprint of the entire universe. Somewhat like when sperm meets the ovum, and when they are — twenty-four, twenty-four — and tangle with each other, and they take the ‹ovum›, they take the shape: so it has a blueprint of all the personality of the child. Whatever will be developed will be developed out of this. That is almost a *citta* stage, *citta*-like stage of embryo.

And so it is a first product of the *prakṛti* and collaboration of *prakṛti* and *puruṣa* — means collaboration of mind and matter. When they collaborate with each other, the first product is mind. Now, mind not in the European sense: mind in the sense of *manas*, mind in the sense of — like I have already explained, subconscious mind, unconscious mind and conscious mind. It has all the three characteristic, because it inherits the *sattva*, *rajas*, *tamo-guṇa* from nature, being a first product of the nature, and it inherit also the consciousness from the side of the consciousness, *puruṣa*.

So *prakṛti* by itself is not conscious. It's simply a creative force. It's a transitory phenomena, all the time getting transformed, constantly transforming phenomena. And the consciousness is a changeless phenomena, without any change. It's a constant, and variables: *puruṣa* is a constant, and *prakṛti* is a with all sorts of variables, infinite variables. But the *citta*, since it's a son of both, so it inherits the quality of both: that is a conscious variable. Not conscious constant, but conscious variables. And therefore whatever we understand, whatever we desire, whatever we do, whatever we act, all informations are there, stored in our *citta*, because it is a blueprint of our understanding. What does not content in your *citta*, it will be very difficult for you to understand. That which is beyond our *citta*, we cannot understand it only on account of the intellect or ego or the agile mind, overactive mind, proactive mind.

# Brief Summary

**Content.** The session resumes the question left open in the previous lectures: whether *kāla* is a twenty-fifth category or falls within the twenty-four. The speaker recalls the opinions — time as the first operativeness of the *guṇas*' equilibrium, time as the *ceṣṭā* of Bhagavān and *puruṣa*, time as the disturbance produced when consciousness is infused into matter — and explains that Kapila taught both a *nirīśvara* and an *īśvara* Sāṅkhya. Against the Western assumption that two doctrines need two authors, he sets the Indian custom of many teachings for many natures (Buddha, the Veda, Śaṅkarācārya, Vyāsa's eighteen Purāṇas), *ekaṁ sad viprā bahudhā vadanti* read as the seers' many descriptions of one many-sided reality rather than the blind men's elephant, and the monopoly of truth, *mama satyam*, as the root of conflict. In the Vedāntic Sāṅkhya *kāla* is one of the five aspects of Akṣara Brahman (*kāla*, *karma*, *svabhāva*, *prakṛti*, *puruṣa*), unmanifest there and manifest in the world like quartz in a watch. The infusion of consciousness into *prakṛti* produces *citta*: individually the unconscious mind, the silent storeroom behind the noisy showroom of intellect and ego; cosmically the blueprint of the universe, like the embryo; a "conscious variable" inheriting passivity from *puruṣa* and activity from *prakṛti*. He compares the empiricists' tabula rasa and the rationalists' innate ideas with Sāṅkhya, explains why this chapter, with the parallel in Mahāprabhuji's *Sarvanirṇaya*, is in the Vedānta syllabus, and shows Mahāprabhuji's threefold *ādhibhautika*/*ādhyātmika*/*ādhidaivika* scheme in wealth and Lakṣmī, speech and Sarasvatī, Gaṇapati with Ṛddhi and Siddhi, Kālī and time, Viṣṇu and *svabhāva*, Brahmā and *karma*. The power of the unconscious is why yoga is *citta-vṛtti-nirodha* and why *sevā* is *cetas tat-pravaṇam*; *manas*, *citta*, *buddhi* and *ahaṅkāra* are four functions of one *antaḥkaraṇa*. Answering students, he explains *kāla* as the first vibration arising from the contact of consciousness and matter. He then reads Śrīmad Bhāgavata 3.26.19–22 with the Subodhinī: the birth of the golden *mahat* from the *puruṣa*'s seed in the disturbed womb of *prakṛti*; *mahat* as seed of the universe drinking the darkness of dissolution; *citta* as Vāsudeva, *saccidānanda*, the *ādhidaivika* form; and its three marks — *svacchatva*, *avikāritva*, *śāntatva* — which mark it off from *buddhi*, *manas* and *ahaṅkāra*, defined by its natural state, as water is by its clearness.
