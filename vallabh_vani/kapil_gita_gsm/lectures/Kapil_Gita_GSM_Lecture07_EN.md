# Brief Summary

**Content.** The session opens with a student's question on what worship means. The speaker answers that every engagement of the person, worship included, draws on three quotients of personality: the emotional quotient of worship is love, the intellectual quotient is *māhātmya-jñāna*, and the behavioural quotient is *sevā*. The three can be engaged in different ratios, and one may be satisfied while another lags. Closing the twenty-fifth chapter, he sets the Subodhinī's three fruits (*sāyujya*, Vaikuṇṭha, *jīvanmukti*) beside the three fruits of *sevā* in Mahāprabhujī's teaching, where *alaukika-sāmarthya* takes the place of *jīvanmukti*; this parallel is his reason for putting the Kapila Gītā in the syllabus. Opening the twenty-sixth chapter with its introductory *kārikās*, he weighs three English words for *ātmā*. Soul is set against body, self against not-self, and spirit against matter. For Sāṅkhya, which lifts the dualism to the metaphysical level, he prefers self or spirit, and matter. He argues that consciousness is not matter from rebirth (it is not bound to one time), from omnipresence, from formlessness (it never becomes a percept) and from the concept, which takes up no space. He then sets Mahāprabhujī's *saṁsāra*, the obsession with I and mine, which is to be worn lightly and washed like a shirt, against Kapila's pair of *saṁsāra* and *mūlabhāva*. In *saṁsāra* the ever-changing *prakṛti* and the never-changing *puruṣa* interact; in *mūlabhāva* they do not. Kapila serves Sāṅkhya and devotion to his mother like *dāl-bhāt*, cooked separately and mixed afterwards, while Vallabhācārya's synthesis of Sāṅkhya and Upaniṣad is a *khichḍī*. Verses 3.26.1–10 are then read. The *puruṣa* is beginningless and self-illumined. Its meeting with *prakṛti* "by *yadṛcchā*" is the weak point of Sāṅkhya: the Veda-stuti objects to it, Śaṅkarācārya answers it by making the interaction beginningless too, and science is in the same position over the Big Bang. The Upaniṣad answers it with a third factor, the *preritā*, so that Brahman is the trio of enjoyer, enjoyed and inspirer. Deluded by *prakṛti*'s products, consciousness takes on the doership of acts that matter performs by itself, and it pays the bill for them: that is bondage. To Devahūti's question Kapila replies with the definition of *prakṛti* as *triguṇa*, *avyakta*, *pradhāna*. The speaker reads the *guṇas* as forces of matter (*rajas* motion, *tamas* stoppage, *sattva* light) and the five elements as five states of matter, from space to solid. In the questions he explains how *prakṛti* is *avyakta* and how the equilibrium of the *guṇas* is disturbed and controlled, and he admits that matter changes by interacting with other matter, as the Cārvāka's example of *kaṅku* shows.
