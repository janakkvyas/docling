# 9. Three Theories of Validity: Correspondence, Coherence and Pragmatism

*[00:54:09]*

**Speaker:** So let us discuss the determination, about the valid knowledge. We had three theories in India — one theory… And in Europe also: theory of correspondence, theory of coherence and pragmatic theory. These three theories in Europe were invented, or upheld, by the western philosopher much, much late, in the medieval period. But in India we had the three different theories about the validity of knowledge, as per recorded history of Indian philosophy, more than two thousand years. Buddha believed in the pragmatic theory of the truth. Some of the philosopher believed in the correspondence theory of the truth, valid knowledge. And some of the philosophers believed in the coherence theory.

So what is correspondence? When your knowledge corresponds to the objective reality, you have a valid knowledge; when it does not correspond… For instance, this is a note of twenty rupees. If this note appears to be as a twenty rupees, then it is a valid appearance, valid knowledge and valid determination. But in your haste, if you consider this as a two hundred rupees, it becomes invalid, because your understanding of this note does not correspond to this piece of paper. So no correspondence, no validity.

Then comes the theory of coherence: whether our knowledge corresponds to the object or not, but if it has a coherence. Because knowledge comes in the succession, like a wave in the river, one after the other. So if one wave is in coherence with the other wave… So like that, in wavy pattern we have a one feeling or one appearance. That appearance coheres with the other appearance, then it is valid; if it does not cohere with the other appearance, then it is invalid. Why snake and rope considered invalid? Why then appearance of snake considered invalid? First of all, correspondence theory would say this is invalid knowledge, illusion, because you are perceiving it to be a snake, but as a matter of fact it is a rope; so it doesn't correspond to the object, that's why it is invalid. Coherence theory teacher would say: no, we have two perception. One perception that we have in darkness, that in darkness rope appeared to be like snake. # Brief Summary

**Content.** The session is given wholly to the epistemology that underlies the Sāṅkhya chapters. The speaker first completes his account of *khyātivāda*: appearance is of the thing as it is or of the thing as it appears, and the latter is a condition of perception, an illusion, or a hallucination; illusions are conditioned (they remain even when the truth is known) or unconditioned (they vanish once the truth is known), while hallucination is a fabrication of the mind with no object. Conditions of perception such as perspective are not errors, since we are not confused by them. Answering a student, he then argues that *pramāṇa* means valid knowledge, not true knowledge: validity is conformity to agreed norms, so a false appearance may be valid and a true one invalid, and which stage of *pratyakṣa* one takes as valid (sensation, perception, determination or recognition) is a technical choice. Validity follows our purpose, not reality, as the case of a devotee who feared *nitya-līlā* shows. He sets out the correspondence, coherence and pragmatic theories, with their Indian definitions, and says that Buddha held the pragmatic theory and accepted only sensation, as did Śaṅkarācārya, while Vallabhācārya takes determination as valid and uses all three theories in tiers, each where verification allows. Reading Mahāprabhujī's text, he explains *niścaya* as *yathārthānubhava*, the object being half of knowledge; memory is not determination for want of an object; inference is produced by the object through a connecting term; analogy is perception aided by verbal information and not an independent *pramāṇa*; and *śabda* is independent because its impressions need no resemblance. Vallabhācārya's three *pramāṇas* are valid for practical life but do not give Brahman, which is known first through scripture alone (*pramāṇaṁ tac-catuṣṭayam*), after which everything perceived, inferred or heard is Brahman. The mind is a *pramāṇa* only when disciplined by yoga, memory is not a *pramāṇa* except as the memory of Ṭhākurjī, and the sense that the body is the self is a conditioned perception.
