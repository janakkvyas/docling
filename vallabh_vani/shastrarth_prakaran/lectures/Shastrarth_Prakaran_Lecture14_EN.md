# Brief Summary

**Philosophical Content.** The session takes up again kārikās 23 and 31, by which avidyā gives the jīva saṁsāra and vidyā and avidyā are Hari's powers working on the jīva alone, not on jaḍa or the antaryāmī, and sets out to show their procedure, their prakriyā. In the Saccidānanda Brahman the tirobhāva of ānanda separates the cid-aṁśa; the individual puruṣa, the jīva, is joined with prakṛti and its tattvas, and adhyāsa follows. Pure consciousness has neither ahantā nor mamatā; once acquired, ahantā grows into anger, hatred and jealousy, and mamatā into desire, rāga and greed. The defect is not in the seed but in the growth, which the speaker calls cancerous, and this growth is what yields duḥkhitva and anīśatā. Bare consciousness, left when ānanda is withdrawn, is neither knowledge nor ignorance: it illumines whatever comes, so ignorance spreads over it, while knowledge can bear even avijñāna. Ahantā and kāma act like a car's accelerator and brake: where desire grows pride gives way, and where pride grows desire gives way. With ānanda go Bhagavān's six qualities, which in him are never lost (Kṛṣṇa's avilupta jñāna, which fears neither killing nor dying and enjoys sorrow as one enjoys a tragedy). In the jīva their tirodhāna yields dīnatā (aiśvarya), duḥkhitā (vīrya), hīnatā (yaśas), the calamities of birth, old age and death (śrī), ahaṅkārika moha or solipsism (jñāna) and desire for objects (vairāgya). These results are blameless in themselves; the defect enters at a third step, when each lack is psychologically compensated by one of the six vices, as the speaker's own verse lays out. Mahāprabhuji's positive Brahmavāda, in which the jīva is an aṁśa of Brahman and *amṛtasya putrāḥ*, is the cure for the dualists' sense of lowness. Avidyā's five limbs are svarūpājñāna and the adhyāsas on antaḥkaraṇa (beginning with ahaṅkāra), prāṇa, senses and body. Vidyā's five limbs (kārikā 45cd–46ab) answer them: Sāṅkhya's inventory against svarūpājñāna, yoga's turning inward against antaḥkaraṇādhyāsa, tapas as training in discomfort against prāṇādhyāsa, vairāgya against indriyādhyāsa, and vidyātmikā bhakti, a bhakti of commitment rather than of sneha, against dehādhyāsa. Vairāgya is victory over the object, tyāga an admission of defeat by it; true vairāgya is not being shaken in sorrow nor craving pleasure, and the Jābāla rule puts vairāgya before renunciation. Vidyā and avidyā are not contradictory but complementary and cyclical, like sleep and waking or the Tao circle: each vidyā hides an avidyā (the inventory needs an inventor, the seeker of ātma-darśana an "I", tapas a prāṇa held as one's own, bhakti a body to do bhajana with). In the questions the speaker holds that avidyā did not attach through karma, so karma cannot remove it; neither vidyā nor avidyā can be removed by the jīva, only brought under control, as one can control one's skeleton but not delete it. Consciousness and understanding are separate: one may understand without being conscious and be conscious without understanding. Our cit is bare cit, a product of a mixture, like the lotus out of mud, and the excess of purity that doctors and the maryādīs demand is not what the one who does sevā needs.
