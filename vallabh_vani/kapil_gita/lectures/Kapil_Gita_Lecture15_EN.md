# Brief Summary

**Content.** The closing revision session returns to the twenty-fourth adhyāya as the introduction to the Kapila Gītā. The speaker first sets out why the Kapila Gītā matters for Vallabha Vedānta — it supplies the metaphysics, ontology and epistemology on which the Brahmasambandha ("religion", *religare*) and the theology (*śāsti iti śāstram*) rest — and recalls the chapter's two topics, mokṣa and buddhi, and the ṛṇatraya that must be paid before one may strive for liberation. Kardama's consolation of the despondent Devahūti leads to the central point of the revision: Mahāprabhuji's teaching on sādhana and phala. When Bhagavān has himself announced that he will come, the sādhanas are no longer means of reaching him; they make him known (*bodhayanti*), make him truly attained (*prāpnuvanti*), bring him under the devotee's control (*vaśīkurvanti*) and even produce him in the form the devotee's bhāva gives him (*utpādayanti*) — the reversal of order that distinguishes Puṣṭi-bhakti from the calculative maryādā-mārga, and that must be relished, since one who does not relish his sādhana will never be a bhakta. The five sādhanas for bhagavat-prasāda (dama, niyama, tapas, draviṇa-dāna, śraddhā) are then explained word by word for the examination, and Kapila's birth "as fire from the wood" is used to distinguish prākaṭya from the janma of the time-bound jīva. Against the Śaṅkarite view of a single true form and false appearances, the speaker expounds the Subodhinī's position that both the Vedic forms and the bhakta-bhāvita forms of Bhagavān are equally ānandamaya, since he is bounded neither by place, time nor svarūpa; Kardama's verse *paraṁ pradhānaṁ puruṣaṁ mahāntam* is glossed term by term (kāla as guṇa-kṣobhaka, kavi as Brahmā, trivṛt as ahaṅkāra, svacchanda-śakti as power under the control of his will). Kapila's own statement of purpose follows: Sāṅkhya as the enumeration of the tattvas for ātma-darśana, needed because the body, the ātmā's instrument, becomes a durāśaya that governs its master — a danger the speaker extends to modern technology. Sāṅkhya names and defines (uddeśa, lakṣaṇa) the tattvas so that, by elimination, what is "neither prakṛti nor vikṛti" is known as the puruṣa; it begins from bheda-vyavahāra without contradicting the non-dual śruti, and it is not a new doctrine but the revival of an anādi-siddha path lost through time. Students' questions clarify that *ātmānaṁ nāśayati* means not destruction of the immortal ātmā but the frustration of its purpose — ātma-ghāta rather than ātmoddhāra — parallel to the bhakti-nāśa of service without devotion in the Bhaktivardhinī; that *mitho miśrībhūta* means tattvas mixed together beyond the reach of the senses, which is why Sāṅkhya functions like chemistry's classification table. The session ends with arrangements for the remaining class, the examination's compulsory fifty MCQs, the advice not to by-heart but to understand, and the submission of projects by email.
