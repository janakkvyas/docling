# 5. Nyāya's Objective Realism and Sāṅkhya's Subjective Realism: the Convex Lens

*[00:16:30]*

**Speaker:** Main aspect here described — here the counting is there, but the thing is that which is described by Jeje here is the difference between Nyāya and the Sāṅkhya. How is Nyāya? Nyāya is objective realism and Sāṅkhya is subjective realism. What is objective realism and subjective realism? Objective realism of Nyāya is that — which I think we have discussed earlier, I don't remember — but in Nyāya what is said: there is a *dravya*, material, in which the quality is there, in the material. So qualities is there in the material — is the objective realism. There is a substance, and in that substance qualities — it possesses quality. So Nyāya has come up with the unique idea that when any — *utpannaṁ dravyaṁ kṣaṇam aguṇaṁ tiṣṭhati* — that is, first substance is produced, material substance is produced, and that, for fraction of second, just remains without any quality; then quality is produced there. So just like we live in our place, our home, like that the qualities reside in the substance. That is the objective realism.

And what is the *darśana* of Sāṅkhya? *Darśana* is the subjective realism. What it thinks — means, in case of heat, you can say that substance is hot, means quality of being hot resides in that substance; that is the Nyāya view. But the view of Sāṅkhya is that that *tanmātrā* — whatever qualities are there, you unite that quality, and when you unite that quality, that turns out to be gross material, or substance. Just like that: if sun rays are there, if you take the convex glass, convex mirror — if you take convex mirror or convex lens, and to concentrate that light, sun's rays, on one particular paper part, then what will happen? It will start burning. Sun is just burning phenomena; from there rays are produced, its quality. So dispersed substance is the quality, and again when you unite that dispersed quality, it turns out to be substance. This is the subjective realism. So *tanmātrā* — *tanmātrā*, "that only"; but "that only" means rays: it possesses the heat, and when that rays you unite again, you can produce the fire again. So quality and substance — means hotness and fire — are not two distinct thing; but that hotness, when it is spread out, it is the quality, *tanmātrā*; but when you combine it together, unite it and focus it and make it at one point, it unites and make the substance, the fire. So fire and quality are the — the gross substance and dispersed qualities: the substance, when it is dispersed, comes out to be quality; quality, when united together, combined together, makes the substance. That is the Sāṅkhya *mata*.

From different definition you will understand the difference between — so *antaḥkaraṇa* is one, but it is *catur-granthi*‹?›, means just like a prism has got three faces and the base of it, so according — the cube has got six different faces. Same way the *antaḥkaraṇa*, according to its function and according to its qualities, it is four-faceted, which is *manas*, *buddhi*, *ahaṅkāra* and *citta*. As they have explained, that there is no plural of "I." Feeling of "I" is within me only, because it is *antaḥkaraṇa*; it unites me. "I" and "you" combine to make "we," but there is no possibility of plural of "I." That I-ness is each one; that is *antaḥkaraṇa*. Inside there is a one uniting phenomena which unites everything. So *manas*, *buddhi*, *ahaṅkāra*, and innermost, which do not come in direct contact with the outside world — it communicates, or it receives, or it gives output only through *manas*, *buddhi* — *citta* is the *citta*. # Brief Summary

**Content.** The session opens with a student's query from the Hindi class about the nineteenth and twenty-third ślokas of the twenty-sixth adhyāya. Reading the Subodhinī, the speaker explains how *mahat-tattva* is produced: *kāla* disturbs the equilibrium of *prakṛti*, which becomes the field where *puruṣa* is reflected, as sperm and ovum form the embryo; the *jagad-aṅkura* holds the blueprint of the universe as a seed holds the tree, and it is *hiraṇmaya* because *sat* and *ānanda* combine in it. He lays down the four-fold scheme in which every element will now be defined — production, *ādhibhautika*, *ādhyātmika*, *ādhidaivika*. Resuming at the tenth śloka, he enumerates the twenty-four *prādhānika* elements (five, five, ten, four), contrasts Nyāya's objective realism, in which qualities reside in a substance, with Sāṅkhya's subjective realism, in which a substance is united *tanmātrā*, and expounds the four-faceted *antaḥkaraṇa*, with *ahaṅkāra* as the uniting "I" that has no plural. The question of time follows: *kāla* may be counted as a twenty-fifth; one Sāṅkhya view makes it the first modification of *prakṛti*, another the effect of *puruṣa* on *prakṛti*, by which the pure witness becomes *kartā*, then *bhoktā*, *ahaṅkāra-vimūḍha* and afraid of *kāla*.
