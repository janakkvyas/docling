# Brief Summary

**Content.** The ninth session opens the twenty-sixth adhyāya, which Mahāprabhuji's kārikā assigns to Sāṅkhya taught for *ātmānātma-viveka*. The speaker frames Sāṅkhya as the metaphysics, the *tattva-darśana*, of Vallabha Vedānta, and begins with the *ātmā* as self — the one thing nobody doubts, since even universal doubt presupposes the doubter. He then sets two ways of self-knowledge side by side: the Upaniṣadic way of synthesis, which unifies everything into Brahman and demands systematic Vedāntic training and *śravaṇa–manana–nididhyāsana*, and the analytical way of Sāṅkhya, which, like a botanist with a forest, classifies, names (*nirukti*) and defines (*lakṣaṇa*) the *tattva*s so that *ātmā* and *anātmā* can be told apart. The *nirīśvara* Sāṅkhya stops there; the *seśvara* Sāṅkhya of the Bhāgavata goes further, to the distinction of *jīva* and Brahma, *puruṣa* and Puruṣottama, with *puruṣa* and *prakṛti* as the two beginningless roots of *saṁsāra*. The practical point of the analysis is freedom from *dehātma-bhāva*: the confusion of body and consciousness in which the property of one is attributed to the other. Kapila's opening verses are then read: knowledge of the *tattva*s by which the *puruṣa* is freed from the *guṇa*s of *prakṛti* (3.26.1); *ātma-darśana* as *jñāna* for *niḥśreyasa*, cutting the knot of the heart, which the speaker reads as inner confusion and indecision (3.26.2); and the definition of *puruṣa* as *anādi*, *nirguṇa*, beyond *prakṛti*, shining within, self-luminous, and that by which the whole world is comprehended, each epithet excluding one candidate — body, *prakṛti*, *kāla*, *ahaṅkāra* (3.26.3). Consciousness alone is *sva-para-prakāśaka*; even the *antaḥkaraṇa*, *manas*, *buddhi*, *citta*, is subtle matter. Finally 3.26.4 explains how the all-capable *vibhu* takes up the subtle, divine, *guṇamayī prakṛti* by chance and in *līlā*; against Buddha's doctrine of *kriyā* without *kartā*, Sāṅkhya keeps its two principles, and the closing recapitulation shows them cooperating as the lame man and the blind man, as machine and energy, in a play begun for no reason but play.
